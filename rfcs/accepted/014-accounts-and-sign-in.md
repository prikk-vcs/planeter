# RFC 014 — Accounts and sign-in: who a person is, how they prove it, and what they can manage themselves

**Status.** **Accepted 2026-11-25** (the owner: "Approved.", after the review below; the six open questions are settled as the architect recommended: Q1 the handle rule as written, Q2 an embedded common-password list, Q3 a required expiry of 90 days by default and 365 at most, Q4 a private certificate authority later and separately, Q5 organizations' lifecycle as RFC 015 after step 2, Q6 disable only, never delete). Handoffs: `handoffs/014-accounts-and-sign-in/` (`accounts-foundation-handoff-v1.md` first). Proposed and revised 2026-11-25 after the owner's review ("Halfly accepted. Review them carefully with my fundamental philosophy": the direction, the three steps and sui-id's place stand; every decision was re-read against the code and against the philosophy, and **eight defects of the first draft were found and corrected**, listed at the end under "What the review changed"). Step 1 (0.10.0) is open for implementation; steps 2 and 3 follow their own handoffs. **D-12 (the interoperability test) is a handoff of its own, after step 1's operator surface.**
**Tracks.** ROADMAP "After 0.9.0"; STATUS IS-66, IS-2, IS-9, IS-8; threat model `RR-10`, `RR-11`, `T-7`, new `T-22`; RFC 002 (the `authorize()` contract and the credential model), RFC 012 (words, accessibility), RFC 013 (the pattern of `planeter operator`); SCHEDULE D-105, D-106.
**Touches.** `planeter-auth` (accounts: a disabled state; tokens: a name, a creation time, a last use; the account event record), `planeter-store`/`planeter-auth` SQLite (three idempotent changes), `planeter-web` (the settings pages, the sign-in page, the first-sign-in page), `planeter` (the `operator account …` family; the issuer allowance), `planeter-core` (the egress policy for one host), docs (`docs/accounts.md`), the Japanese. **Not here:** an identity provider inside planeter, a second factor, an email sender, a password-reset by mail (each is a provider's job: D-1); organizations' lifecycle (create, rename, delete: Open question 5); account deletion (D-6); SSH-key and deploy-key pages (RFC 004's transport owns them; later).
**Evidence.** The code as read on 2026-11-25: accounts (`user`, `display_name`, `email`, `password_hash`), `tokens` (a hash, a user, an access level, repositories, an expiry: **no name, no creation time, no last use, no way for a person to see or revoke one**), `sessions` (7 days), `oidc_links`, **no function that creates an account outside tests**, and an unlinked OIDC identity is refused with "SsoUnlinked" (RFC 002: no auto-provisioning); a handle accepts upper case and `@ + ~` (`check_owner_handle`) and an owner is matched **exactly** (`alice` and `Alice` are two owners); the egress guard refuses loopback and private targets and no setting loosens it; the three projects of the owner under `.git-exclude/projects/` (sui-id: EdDSA-only ID tokens, `client_secret_basic|post|none`, claims `email`, `email_verified`, `preferred_username`, `name`, an https issuer); ledger PK-90/91 are not involved.

## Summary

Today a person **cannot become an account, cannot get a token, and cannot change a password** without someone editing the database: the 0.8.0 end-to-end run seeded SQLite by hand, and the client's documentation says how to present a token, not how to get one. RFC 014 closes that in three steps, each releasable alone, **without turning planeter into an identity provider**: (1) **the operator's surface** (`planeter operator account …`: create, list, set a password, disable, enable, link an OIDC identity, grant a repository role), the one narrow allowance that lets an identity provider on a private address be reached, and the provenance record of every account act; (2) **self-service pages** (tokens with a name, a scope and an expiry, shown once and revocable; password change; the person's sessions; a log of what happened to their account); (3) **provisioning and collaborators** (an operator's choice to create an account on first sign-in; repository administrators granting roles on a page). Everything that is hard to do safely and that a provider already does — a second factor, recovery by mail, breach checks, lockout policy — **stays with the provider**; planeter's own passwords remain for the operator who has no provider, with an honest limit (no second factor, RR-10) and a switch to turn them off.

## Why not build it all in, and why not do nothing

*Do nothing* leaves a forge that cannot be used by anyone who cannot edit SQLite: the owner's own words are "clean, safe and secure, robust": a system whose first user needs a database editor is none of those. *Build an identity provider in* would give planeter a password-reset by mail, a second factor, a recovery flow, an email queue and an abuse surface that a small single-binary forge cannot keep true; the owner already runs one that does (sui-id). So planeter keeps what it must own (**who an account is, what it may do, what machine credentials it has**) and delegates what a provider owns (**proving a person is who they say, and recovering them when they cannot**).

## Decisions

- **D-1 — planeter is an OIDC relying party and holds no identity-provider function.** No second factor, no email, no password reset by mail, no "forgot password" page, no account recovery flow, no breach-password lookup (that last would send hashes of secrets to a third party: refused on principle). A person who cannot sign in is helped by **the provider** (for SSO accounts) or **the operator** (for a local password: D-3). Stated on the sign-in page and in `docs/accounts.md` in one sentence each.
- **D-2 — An account is: a handle, a display name, an optional verified email for display, a state, and how it signs in.** The **handle** is the account's identity and its namespace (`/alice/forge`). **New handles follow one rule** (Open question 1 proposes it): ASCII lowercase letters, digits, `-`, `_` and `.` only, start with a letter or digit, 1–39 characters, **not in the server's reserved words** (the existing reserved set), **unique case-insensitively across accounts and organizations** (so a person can never be confused with another by case), and **never changed** (a rename would break every URL and clone address: not in this RFC). Existing mixed-case handles keep working and are listed by the first start of the release that carries this (`planeter operator account list --check` shows any that collide case-insensitively with another). The **display name** is text (display-safe by the project's cut rules, at most 100 characters). **`email`** is stored only if the provider says `email_verified`, is shown only to the account's holder and to the operator, **and is never used to find, link or merge an account** (linking by email is the classic account-takeover: a provider that lets a person claim an unverified address, or a second provider with a laxer rule, would hand over the account). Linking is **only** by the provider's stable `(issuer, subject)` pair.
- **D-3 — The operator's surface: `planeter operator account …` and `token …` (step 1, 0.10.0).** The family `planeter operator` exists since 0.9.0 (`key add`). **One rule of that command does not carry over, and the first draft was wrong to say it did:** `key add` needs the server **stopped** because it writes a repository, and the repository lease lives inside the server process. **An account is a row of the database, not a repository**, and onboarding a person is routine, not an emergency: asking an operator to stop a forge each time someone joins would be a trap. So **the account commands run beside a running server**: they touch only SQLite (already in WAL mode with a 5 s busy timeout, shared by readers and writers of both processes), each in one transaction (`BEGIN IMMEDIATE`), and the server reads accounts, sessions and tokens from the database at every request (no cache to invalidate: *a test shows a disabled account's live session and token refused on the very next request*). If the database stays locked past the timeout the command says so and changes nothing. They still never read a secret of the server, never take a password or a token from a command line, are translated, have exit codes, and are never silent (each act writes an account event, D-7). Commands:
  - `account create HANDLE --name TEXT [--password-prompt]`. A password is **read from the terminal** without echo, twice (or from a file descriptor for scripts, `--password-fd N`; never an argument, never an environment variable), checked by D-4, stored as Argon2id. Without `--password-prompt` the account has **no password** and signs in only through a linked identity.
  - `account set-password HANDLE`: the same prompt; ends all the account's sessions, nothing else.
  - `account disable HANDLE --reason TEXT` and `account enable HANDLE` (D-6). **`disable` first prints the repositories for which this account is the only administrator** ("these will have no administrator until you grant one") so that an offboarding does not orphan anything silently; it still proceeds.
  - `account link-oidc HANDLE --issuer URL --subject TEXT` (IS-2; the issuer must be the configured one) and `account unlink-oidc`.
  - `account grant OWNER/NAME HANDLE --role read|write|maintain|admin` and `account revoke OWNER/NAME HANDLE`. **The help and the result say each role in one plain sentence** (read: look and clone; write: also propose work; maintain: also ask for a delivery and review; admin: also manage who has access), and `grant` prints the **display name** it granted to, so a mistyped handle is seen at once.
  - **`token create HANDLE --name TEXT --scope read-only|write|full --expires-days N [--out FILE]`, `token list HANDLE`, `token revoke HANDLE ID`.** **Without these, step 1 ends with a person who has an account and cannot use `planeter-client`, which needs a token; the first draft left that gap to step 2.** The token is printed once to the terminal or written to `--out` (mode 0600, refused if the file exists), never logged, never stored but as its hash; an expiry is required (D-8); the event says "created by the operator" so the holder sees who made it. This is the bootstrap, and the way an operator-only deployment (no provider, no pages yet) works; step 2 adds the person's own page for the same thing.
  - `account list [--check]` and `account show HANDLE`: read-only; they print no hash and no secret.
  Each refusal changes nothing; each success prints the sentence the account's holder will see in their log (D-7).
- **D-4 — The password rule, for local passwords.** **At least 12 and at most 128 Unicode characters (scalar values, after normalization)**; no composition rules (they teach people worse passwords); not equal to the handle; **not in a small embedded list of the most common passwords** (Open question 2: the list's size and source, which must be a licence-clean list kept in the repository, never fetched); **normalized to Unicode NFKC before it is counted, checked and hashed** (the same on setting and on signing in): the same Japanese password typed through different input methods or on different systems is not always the same sequence of code points (a composed and a decomposed form of one character), and without normalization a person would be locked out of their own password by their keyboard: for ASCII, NFKC changes nothing, so every existing password is unaffected. The normalizer is `icu_normalizer`, **already in the lock** (reached through `url`), so this is a direct dependency on a crate the tree already trusts, with its own dependency-policy record, not a new crate; compared in constant time as today; the error says which rule failed and what to do, in one sentence, never what the stored value is. Argon2id parameters are the existing ones; **a rehash on sign-in when the parameters are raised** is part of this RFC (so the parameters can be raised without a migration).
- **D-5 — Local passwords can be switched off.** `PLANETER_LOCAL_PASSWORDS=off` (**default `on`**, one rule; recommended `off` when SSO is configured): the sign-in page shows only the provider; the password form route answers as if there were no password sign-in; **existing password hashes are kept** (switching back on restores them). With it off and the provider down nobody can sign in through the web: the operator's commands and a restart with it `on` are the way back, and the start-up line says which is set. This is how an operator gets a second factor without planeter building one: the provider's. `docs/accounts.md` says so in two sentences and RR-10 is restated: *a local password account has no second factor*.
- **D-6 — An account is disabled, never deleted.** Deleting an account would orphan its repositories' namespace, its proposals, its comments and its audit trail, and would free a handle for someone else to take (an impersonation). **Disable** instead: the account cannot sign in (a **password** sign-in answers with the same coarse "could not sign you in" as any bad credential: no oracle, IS-31; **a sign-in through the provider, whose identity the provider has just proved, is told plainly** — "This account has been disabled. Ask the person who runs this server." — because telling a person who has proved who they are that their own account is disabled reveals nothing to anyone else, and a coarse refusal would leave them confused and asking why), **its sessions end at once, and every token it holds is refused** (checked at each use, not only at creation); its repositories, proposals and comments remain and show its handle; **the handle is never reused**. `enable` restores it (its tokens work again until their expiry). The reason is recorded and shown to the account's holder in their log (D-7) and to the operator, **not to other people**. The operator may also **archive** nothing: there is no deletion of a person's history in this RFC (a privacy request is an operator-and-owner matter and is written into `docs/accounts.md` as such, not automated).
- **D-7 — Every account act leaves an event the account's holder can read.** A table `account_events` (account, kind, when, a short detail, who: `self | operator | provider`): sign-in by a new method, a password set or changed, a token created or revoked, a session ended, an identity linked or unlinked, a role granted or revoked, disabled or enabled (with the reason). **Never a secret, a token, a hash or an IP address** (the project's log rules; IPs stay in the transient throttle only). The holder sees the last 50 on their security page (step 2); the operator sees them with `account show`. Events are kept (they are the audit trail of the account) and bounded by a retention number the operator may set; the default keeps everything (a few hundred bytes an event).
- **D-8 — Tokens a person can see, name, expire and revoke (step 2, 0.11.0).** The page `Settings → Access tokens` (and the API under `/api/v1/account/tokens`, the same rules): create with a **name** (1–60 characters, so the person can tell them apart), a **scope** (`read-only | write | full`, as today, with the words the existing pages use), the repositories (all of the person's access, or chosen ones) and an **expiry that is required** (default 90 days, at most 365; no token without an end). The token is **shown once**, on the page that creates it, with a copy-friendly box and one sentence ("It cannot be shown again; keep it somewhere private"); only its hash is stored (as today). The list shows name, scope, repositories, created, **last used (at most once an hour, so reading is not a write on every request)** and expiry, and a **Revoke** button (CSRF-protected, no confirmation page: revoking is safe and undoable by creating another). **A token can never create or revoke tokens** and cannot change a password (these routes accept a signed-in **session** only: a stolen token cannot mint its successor). **Creating a token needs a recent sign-in:** a session no older than 15 minutes, else the person is asked to sign in again and comes back to the form. The page the person returns to is **one of a fixed list of local paths named in the code, never a value from the request** (no open redirect). *What this proves, said exactly:* that a sign-in happened in planeter within 15 minutes, which defeats a **stolen old session cookie**; it does **not** prove the provider asked for the password again (a provider may satisfy it from its own live session); whether sui-id honours a request to re-authenticate (`prompt=login` / `max_age`) is **measured by the interoperability test (D-12)** and, if it does, planeter asks for it and the sentence gets stronger — until then the page does not claim more. **Every page and response that shows a token secret carries `Cache-Control: no-store` and `Referrer-Policy: no-referrer`, and the secret is in no URL, no log and no event.** `full` tokens say in plain words what they allow (including delivering a branch) and that they are what a maintainer's `planeter-client deliver` needs.
- **D-9 — Passwords and sessions, from the person's side (step 2).** *Change password* (current password, new password twice; ends **all other** sessions; the page exists only when the account has a password and local passwords are on). *Sessions:* a list (**when it started and when it was last used, and which one is this**: nothing else; **planeter stores no browser label and no address for a session**, so there is no tracking data to protect, to leak or to misread) with **End** for each and **End all others**. *Sign out* is as today. All pages are server-rendered, work without script, have labels on every field and the right `autocomplete` values (`current-password`, `new-password`), do not block paste, and are checked by the structural rules in both languages. Failed attempts count against the existing per-account and per-address throttles; the new routes get their own per-account limit.
- **D-10 — Provisioning and collaborators (step 3, 0.12.0, designed here, built last).** *Provisioning:* `PLANETER_OIDC_PROVISION=off|create`, default **off** (the 0.8.0 behaviour: an unlinked identity is refused). With `create`, a verified identity with no link is taken to **one page: "Choose your handle"** (the provider's `preferred_username` offered as a suggestion, **checked against D-2's rule, never used unchecked**; the display name from `name`; nothing else), which creates the account and links the identity in one step, **with no repository role and no organization membership** (a person may create repositories in their own namespace, as today; they get no access to anyone else's). The page is bound to the sign-in: after the provider's verified answer the server keeps **one short-lived pending record** (random id in a cookie, the verified issuer and subject, the suggestion; **10 minutes**, used once), so a person cannot choose a handle for an identity they did not just prove, and a replay finds nothing. The handle's uniqueness is held by the database (a race loses cleanly with "that handle is taken"). **A cap bounds what a provider can create:** `PLANETER_OIDC_PROVISION_MAX` accounts (default **200**; the server says in the start-up line and on the page when it is reached), because an open provider otherwise lets anyone fill the instance; the existing per-user repository quota applies to every provisioned account unchanged. An operator may also **allow only one provider-side domain or group** later (not in this RFC). *Collaborators:* a repository's administrators (the role `Admin`, who already own "grants" in RFC 002) get a page `Repository → Access` listing who holds which role and letting them **grant and revoke** `read|write|maintain` (**each with its plain sentence on the page**, as in D-3; never `admin`: the operator grants admin; an administrator cannot make another administrator or remove the last one), by **handle** (exact; the page confirms the display name before granting, to avoid a mistyped handle granting a stranger). Every grant is an account event for the person granted.
- **D-11 — The identity provider on a private address, with one narrow allowance.** The egress guard (RFC 004) refuses loopback, private, link-local and CGNAT targets for every outbound request, and no setting loosens it, so **a provider on the same host or network is unreachable today**. `PLANETER_OIDC_ALLOW_PRIVATE_ISSUER=on` permits private targets **only for the configured issuer's host**, and **only if every endpoint the provider's discovery names (authorization, token, JWKS, userinfo) has that same host**: any other host in discovery is refused as today. The egress guard already **pins the addresses it resolved** and connects only to them (so DNS cannot rebind between the check and the connection): the allowance applies to those pinned addresses of that one host and nothing else. The scheme stays **https** (a plain `http` issuer is refused, as now). The start-up line says that the allowance is on and for which host; `docs/accounts.md` says what it risks (the provider's host can be a private service: the operator is the one who set the issuer, ASSUME-1). Open question 4 asks whether the provider may use a private certificate authority (a CA file) and how.
- **D-12 — sui-id: preferred, watched, tested before it is recommended.** Nothing in planeter names sui-id in code. **A handoff of its own** (not scheduled before step 1 is accepted) builds an **interoperability test** against a real sui-id release in a scratch environment: discovery; the authorization-code flow with PKCE S256; EdDSA ID-token verification; the nonce; the claims `sub`, `email`, `email_verified`, `preferred_username`, `name`; `client_secret_basic` and `post`; a provider with a key rotation between two sign-ins; an expired and a replayed code; and the provider answering slowly or not at all (planeter must fail closed and say so). The result, measured, goes into the ledger (a new PK row) and into `docs/accounts.md` as **"tested with sui-id {version} on {date}"**, nothing stronger. The pins review of each release gets one row, **"preferred identity provider"**: its latest release, its own statement of production readiness, advisories on it and its dependencies. **A provider other than sui-id works if it speaks OpenID Connect as above; planeter claims nothing about one it has not tested.**

## What does not change

`authorize()` and the credential model (RFC 002: a token's scope is the intersection with the user's permissions); the keyless forge (INV-2, ENF-2): no account or sign-in act touches a signing key or a repository's history; the maintainer-keys work (RFC 013): **a repository role (`maintain`, `admin`) and a maintainer's signing key are different things and stay different** (granting someone `maintain` lets them ask for a delivery; it does not make their key trusted: keys are RFC 013's, added by the operator); the delivery rule; the 404-for-hidden-repositories rule; the rate limits and the coarse sign-in failure.

## Threats (draft of T-22)

- **Account takeover by linking.** Linking by email, by `preferred_username` or by a claim other than the provider's stable subject: **refused by D-2** (a test with a provider that returns another person's verified email and a changed `preferred_username` and the same issuer: the unlinked identity is not linked, and provisioning (D-10) makes a new account, never merges).
- **A stolen token mints its successor, changes a password, or ends sessions.** Not possible: those routes accept a session only (D-8, D-9); a token's reach is the existing `authorize()` capped by its scope. *Test: every account route with a token of every scope: refused.*
- **A stolen session creates a token.** Needs a recent sign-in (15 minutes) and a CSRF token; the token is shown once; the creation is an account event the holder sees. Residual: a thief with a live, recent session can create one token before the holder notices (RR-22).
- **The operator is attacked through the command.** Passwords never on a command line or in the environment; the command needs the data and the server stopped; the account events say what was done.
- **Handle confusion and squatting.** One lowercase rule, case-insensitive uniqueness, reserved words, never reused after disable; existing mixed-case handles are reported, not silently renamed.
- **A provider that is compromised or malicious.** It can sign in as any account linked to it and, with provisioning on, create accounts; it cannot reach a repository role it was not granted, a signing key, or history. Provisioning is off by default; an operator who turns it on accepts that the provider decides who exists (RR-22).
- **Password guessing.** Argon2id, the per-account and per-address throttles (in memory, per process: RR-11), a 12-character minimum and a common-password list; with SSO configured the operator can turn local passwords off (D-5).
- **The operator command beside a running server (D-3).** Two processes write one SQLite file: WAL, one transaction per act, a 5 s timeout and a refusal that changes nothing; the server re-reads accounts, sessions and tokens at each request. *Tests: a command run while the server serves requests (a disabled account's session and token refused at once; a created account able to sign in at once; a held write lock giving the timeout sentence).*
- **Oracle: does an account exist?** The sign-in answer stays coarse and equal in time (IS-31); a disabled account answers like a wrong password; the operator command may say a handle exists (the operator already can read the database).
- **The issuer allowance (D-11) used for server-side request forgery.** One host, https only, every discovered endpoint on that host, set by the operator (not by a request); the other private ranges stay refused. *Tests: a provider whose discovery names a second private host (refused), a redirect to a private address (refused), the allowance off (as today).*
- **Cost.** Account events bounded in size and per account list reads (50); token and session lists bounded (200 tokens per account; the operator can change the number); the `last used` write at most hourly.
- **Residual (RR-22).** A local password account has no second factor (RR-10 unchanged); a thief of a live recent session can mint one token; an operator who enables provisioning delegates who exists to the provider; a mistyped handle in the operator's `grant` is the operator's responsibility (the command prints the display name it granted to).

## Phasing and releases

- **Step 1 — 0.10.0:** D-2 (the rule and the check), D-3 (the operator family **including the operator's token commands**), D-4 (**with the normalization**), D-5, D-6, D-7 (the events), D-11 (the allowance); `docs/accounts.md` (the operator's half); the schema changes (accounts: `created_at`, `disabled_at`, `disabled_reason`, `password_changed_at`; the events table; **tokens: `id`, `name`, `created_at`, `last_used_at`** added idempotently, existing tokens named "(created before 0.10.0)"); the sui-id interoperability handoff **may run beside it** (D-12). A real person can then be given an account, a role **and a token** without SQLite, while the server keeps running.
- **Step 2 — 0.11.0:** D-8, D-9 (the settings pages and the account API), the rest of `docs/accounts.md`, the Japanese for the owner (the reading is the largest of the three: about 80 messages, to be sized by the handoff).
- **Step 3 — 0.12.0:** D-10 (provisioning, the collaborators page).
Each step is its own set of handoffs, its own review, its own security pass and its own release; the owner may stop after any.

## What the review changed (the first draft's defects, each found by checking it against the code or against the philosophy)
1. **Account commands required the server stopped** (copied from `key add`): wrong for a routine act; they now run beside the server (D-3).
2. **Step 1 left a person with an account and no token** (the client needs one; the self-service page was step 2): the operator's token commands are in step 1 (D-3).
3. **Passwords were not Unicode-normalized**: a Japanese password could differ between input methods and lock its owner out; NFKC with a crate already in the lock (D-4).
4. **A disabled person signing in through the provider was told nothing useful**: the provider has proved who they are, so the answer is now plain; password sign-in stays coarse (D-6).
5. **"Recent sign-in" promised more than it proves**, and its return page could have been an open redirect: the claim is stated exactly and the return paths are a fixed list (D-8).
6. **Sessions would have stored a browser label**: removed; nothing but times is kept (D-9).
7. **Provisioning could be filled by an open provider and needed a pending record the first draft did not describe**: a cap, a 10-minute single-use pending record, the repository quota (D-10).
8. **`disable` could orphan repositories silently**, **roles had no plain-words description**, and **the password default read contradictorily**: `disable` lists what it leaves without an administrator, each role has one sentence, the default is `on` (D-3, D-5).

## Open questions (settled by the owner's "Approved." on 2026-11-25, as recommended)

1. **The handle rule (D-2):** lowercase ASCII only, 1–39 characters, unique case-insensitively, never changed. *Architect's recommendation: yes.* It is stricter than today (which accepts upper case, `@ + ~` and up to 128 characters); existing handles are untouched and reported. The alternative (keep today's wider set) leaves `Alice` and `alice` as two people, which is the confusion the philosophy rules out.
2. **Common-password list (D-4):** a small embedded list (a few thousand entries, licence-clean, in the repository) or none. *Recommendation: embed one* (about 30 KB, no network, the project's "no egress by default"); the alternative is a 12-character minimum alone.
3. **Token lifetime (D-8):** required expiry, default 90 days, maximum 365. *Recommendation: yes*; a token that never ends is the thing a forge should not hand out.
4. **A private certificate authority for the provider (D-11):** when the provider is on a private address it often has a private certificate. *Recommendation: a later, separate decision with its own measurement*: today planeter's confined `curl` verifies with the system store; a `PLANETER_OIDC_CA_FILE` would be one more trusted root for one host, and deserves its own review. Do you want it in step 1, later, or never (the operator puts the CA in the system store)?
5. **Organizations (out of this RFC):** organizations exist in the store (`org_role`) with no lifecycle (no one can create one). *Recommendation: its own RFC after step 2* (RFC 015: create, members, rename); until then repositories live in personal namespaces. Agreed?
6. **Account deletion (D-6):** disable only, never delete, never reuse a handle; a person's request to erase is a manual operator-and-owner matter written in `docs/accounts.md`. *Recommendation: yes.* Is there a legal or personal-data requirement you want designed in now?

## Amendment 1 (2026-11-25, after the sui-id architect's reply: `docs/src/planeter-idp-ledger.md` ID-1…ID-10)

The provider told us, from its source, things that change four decisions. Nothing else changes.
1. **D-10 and D-2: planeter reads no `name` and no `preferred_username` from the provider.** The ID token does not carry them (they live at the userinfo endpoint, ID-2) and they are unbounded text (ID-6). The first-sign-in page is simply "Choose your handle" and "Your name": the person types both, checked by the handle rule and the display-name rule; **no userinfo request is ever made**. (This removes a request, an unbounded input and a suggestion that could mislead.) `email` is stored only when `email_verified` is true; sui-id sends `false` today (ID-1), so nothing is stored from it, and that is correct.
2. **D-8: planeter sends neither `prompt` nor `max_age`** until a tagged provider release honours them (ID-4: sui-id ignores both silently, so sending would suggest a guarantee that does not exist). The page's claim stays the exact one: *a sign-in to planeter within 15 minutes*.
3. **D-11 gets one companion: the JWKS refresh is sent with `Cache-Control: no-cache`** (ID-7: the JWKS is `public, max-age=300`, a shared cache could answer the one refresh on an unknown `kid` with the stale copy, and a sign-in right after a rotation would fail).
4. **New D-13 — the session outlives the provider's, so its lifetime is a setting and the operator disables in both places.** A planeter session lasts 7 days (`SESSION_TTL_SECS`) and is independent of the provider's after sign-in; sui-id has no logout notification (ID-5). **Step 1 adds `PLANETER_SESSION_HOURS`** (default 168 = today; 1 to 720; the start-up line states it; `docs/accounts.md` recommends 24 or fewer when a provider is configured and says to **disable a person in both planeter and the provider**). A periodic **refresh check** (the provider's recommended mechanism: use the refresh token about hourly; `invalid_grant` ends the planeter session) is **not built**: it means storing a refresh token per session (a credential at rest, which for a public client is usable by whoever holds it), an hourly outbound call per active session, and a decision for when the provider is unreachable (fail open and keep the session, or fail closed and sign everyone out). It is **Open question 7, the owner's**, below.
5. **D-12: the interoperability test runs against a tagged release** and has the two added items of ID-10.

### Open question 7 (the owner's): should planeter check, about hourly, that a person still exists at the provider?
*Without it:* offboarding takes effect at planeter within the session lifetime (a setting, as short as the operator likes; the operator can also disable the person in planeter at once). *With it* (a later step, its own handoffs): offboarding takes effect within the hour, at the price of storing refresh tokens (encrypting them at rest needs a key planeter does not have today, so they would sit in plaintext in SQLite), a recurring call per session, and a failure policy. *Architect's recommendation: not now.* A short session lifetime plus disabling in both places is simple, has no new secret at rest and no new failure mode; revisit if the operators of a real deployment find a day too long. Your decision, at any time.
